BHF Deep Dive

BHF, the Botryllus histocompatibility factor, is a 252-residue tunicate protein. phmmer's best hit for it in Swiss-Prot is a viral protein, Vpx, at a weak E-value of 0.16. kmerseek finds the same place on both proteins, but scores it as chance. Everything else kmerseek reports for BHF, shuffled copies of BHF also get.

Step 4 · Why that is not enough

How long must a matching run be before chance stops explaining it?

With two letters, a column matches by chance about half the time (, from the share of hydrophobic residues in BHF and in the targets). A run of L matching columns then happens by chance with probability about pL. This search tries starting pairs (BHF positions × k-mers in the index), so a run of L is expected about N × pL times. Drag the run length.

expected chance count BHF–Vpx (phmmer's alignment): longest run, 26 run needed for fewer than 1 by chance

This is a simple count of unbroken runs. kmerseek also weighs k-mers and extends through mismatches, so its E-values differ in detail, but they agree in scale: 460 to 6_060 for the Vpx regions. phmmer scores the same 54 columns with all 20 amino acids. An identical amino acid can carry up to log220 ≈ 4.3 bits against at most 1 for an h/p match. That is how phmmer reaches E = 0.16 on 28% identical residues, and phmmer itself does not count even that as significant.

Step 5 · The chance control

Does BHF beat a shuffled BHF?

100 copies of BHF were shuffled so each keeps every adjacent pair of residues (a dipeptide shuffle), then searched exactly like BHF. A copy can have no real relative, so whatever it finds is chance. Each grey dot is one copy's best region; the black diamond is BHF's. Pick a low-complexity rule (Step 6 explains them). If BHF were finding something real, the diamond would sit below nearly all the dots.

Step 6 · Low complexity

Which of BHF's regions survive a low-complexity filter?

A run of lysines matches any other polar run, so regions resting on low-complexity stretches are composition matches, not relatives. Three rules, from loosest to strictest:

  1. Region: less than half of the region lies in a BHF stretch.
  2. Shared k-mers, BHF: less than half of the residues under the exact shared k-mers lie in a BHF stretch.
  3. Shared k-mers, both proteins: rule 2 holds on BHF and on the target. This rule decides, and it was fixed before the shuffled copies were read.

Tap a row to see its residues.

Step 7 · Function clues

Zinc fingers, membrane anchors, DNA binding?

For all 15_207 targets of BHF's regions, UniProt lists where their zinc fingers, transmembrane segments, lipid anchors and DNA-binding regions are. A region counts when its span on the target overlaps one. If BHF's best regions meant something, these shares would grow as E falls. They don't, and none of the regions with E < 10 lands on any of them.

Step 8 · Smaller k-mers?

Would k below 17 help?

No index below k = 17 exists for this alphabet, so instead of searching, we counted exact h/p k-mers that BHF shares with Swiss-Prot proteins at k = 11 to 16, and did the same for 100 shuffled copies. A relative would show up as one protein sharing many k-mers. Values are shown as % above or below the copies' median.

What this means

BHF has no relative kmerseek can tell from chance

kmerseek sees the same Vpx match phmmer sees, so the seeds work. But at most 1 bit per column means a run of matching h/p letters must be about 36 long before chance stops explaining it. BHF's best alignment has 26. No tool here finds a convincing relative for BHF in Swiss-Prot minus ascidians: phmmer's best is E = 0.16. A claim that BHF is related to anything has to come from evidence other than these E-values.
