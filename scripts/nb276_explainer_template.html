Notebook 276 · Borreliella burgdorferi P66 and human CD47

Circular Evidence

A 2024 preprint proposes that a loop of the bacterial protein P66 mimics human CD47 to bind the macrophage receptor SIRPα. Searching hard enough found a result. Finding out where the result came from took longer.

One setting out of 76 makes CD47 the single best match in the human proteome for a P66 query. The query was cut from the place P66 happens to align to CD47. Move it to the loop the claim is actually about, and CD47's best rank over 152 settings is 8.

What the claim says

The preprint names a predicted extracellular loop of P66, mature residues 181–187, and two aspartates in it. In the UniProt entry for P66 the signal peptide runs 1–21, so that loop is residues 202–208: QENDKDT, with aspartates at 205 and 207. The claim is that this stretch lets P66 bind SIRPα the way CD47's own surface does.

Structure says no, three times

Shape is the natural way to test a mimicry claim, so that came first. Each comparison needs a background, because both quantities are easy to reach by accident: any immunoglobulin domain resembles CD47, and any seven residues can be laid on any other seven.

A model this uncertain cannot show that P66 resembles CD47, and cannot firmly show that it does not. It is not noise — Foldseek's top hits for it are bacterial outer-membrane β-barrels, which is what P66 is — but no number above should be read as settled.

Sequence found nothing, and that part is not a near miss

A separate line of work searched the two proteins for shared k-mers across 16 reduced alphabets and 27 settings. None of the 326 shared k-mers touches the loop. The reason is not that the search was unlucky. Below is the 36-residue stretch where P66 and CD47 align best, written out with the columns an alphabet calls a match. The longest run of consecutive matching columns is never more than 4, in any alphabet, including a two-letter one where 24 of the 36 columns match. An exact 5-mer cannot be placed anywhere in this stretch, so no k-mer search at k of 5 or more can find it, and nothing that reweights k-mers afterwards can either. Inverse document frequency ranks the matches a search already made; with no match there is nothing to weight. Measured over the whole human proteome, the summed IDF of this stretch's matching 5-mers is 0.00 in every alphabet tried.

Why funcgroups8 sees less than hsdm17, despite having fewer classes to spare

Class count does not decide it. funcgroups8 calls a random pair of residues a match 14.5% of the time, rarer than dayhoff6 at 19%. What decides it is which pairs an alphabet merges. Beyond the 10 identical columns, funcgroups8 accepts only I against L or V. sdm12 and hsdm17 accept those and also K against E, which is worth three more columns. So funcgroups8 fails for the most ordinary reason in biochemistry: it keeps acidic and basic residues in separate classes, as a functional-group alphabet should, while the signal in this stretch is three columns of a P66 lysine against a CD47 glutamate. And the two alphabets that find it most strongly are not independent of each other — they accept the same four non-identical pairs and both come from the same 2000 paper.

Then a search did find something

Exact matching has a floor; seed-and-extend does not. A short seed beside a region extends across it, tolerating mismatches. Run that way, one setting produced a 36-residue ungapped segment: P66 197–232 against CD47 109–144, 92% inside CD47's ectodomain, covering 9 of the 21 residues that touch SIRPα. It passed a test fixed in advance — against 437 human proteins matched to CD47 on length and acidic content, reached its score.

Where that score comes from

On the 29 columns outside the loop, CD47 beats all 437. On the loop's own 7 columns, every one of the matched proteins that reach the loop at all beats CD47. It is the best of 438 on the part of the segment the claim is not about, and the worst of on the part it is.

And this is the part that took longest to see

A search tool ranks a database, so the real question is whether CD47 ever comes out on top of the human proteome for a P66 query. It does, once: in hsdm17, with the 36-residue segment, CD47 scores and no other protein of 19,705 reaches it. Sole first place.

But that query is the window seed-and-extend produced because it aligns to CD47. It is not a stretch of P66 chosen on its own merits. No loop-centred window makes CD47 the sole top hit — 0 of 152 settings. Excluding the settings that tie more than 100 proteins at the top, CD47's best rank from a loop-centred window is 8. The window that does put CD47 first is the one placed where CD47 matches.

Why "rank 1 somewhere" proves nothing on its own

Of 200 arbitrary human proteins put through the same 76 settings, 197 reach rank 1 in at least one of them, with a median best rank of 1. Sweeping settings will promote almost any protein to the top of something. That is why the tests above used a setting fixed in advance and a matched background, and why every rank here is shown next to how many proteins share it. Under the stricter criterion — sole top hit, nobody tied — 0 of the 200 ever qualify from a loop-centred window, and neither does CD47.

Holding CD47 out

The slider changes where the window sits but still fixes it by a rule. The sharper test lets the data choose the window, with CD47 taken out of the room: score every one of P66's 583 windows of 36 residues against the 437 matched human proteins that are not CD47, take the window that scores highest, and only then ask where CD47 ranks for it.

Choose the window with CD47 held out

Both halves of that point in opposite directions, and both are worth keeping. Discovery is circular: no search of P66 against a database would ever surface this window, because held out the data picks a different part of the protein and CD47 sits near the middle of the pack for it. But given the window, the match is specific to CD47: a merely matchable stretch would score well against the held-out set too, and this one is exactly median there while being the proteome's best against CD47.

So the result is hypothesis-confirming, not hypothesis-generating. If you already suspect CD47, the segment is unusually good. Nothing would have handed it to you.

What would make it non-circular

The statistics were never the problem. The prespecified test was properly prespecified, the background was real proteins matched on length and composition, and the segment passed. What was contaminated is the query, and no better null fixes that: the window has to be chosen without reference to CD47.

Three ways to do it, in rising order of what they would prove:

  1. Centre the window on the loop and fix the width in advance. Done above. CD47's best rank is 8 of 19,705, and never sole first. This is the test, and the result does not survive it.
  2. Define the window from P66's own structure, not from an alignment. Take the extracellular loop region the model's topology gives, decided before any CD47 comparison. The model's mean pLDDT is 29, so the topology is weak evidence, which is why this was not run.
  3. Hold CD47 out entirely. Run above. The window chosen without CD47 is elsewhere in the protein, and CD47 ranks 4,696 of 19,705 for it. The result does not survive.

All three have now been answered, and the honest statement is the narrow one: P66 197–232 and CD47 109–144 share a real 36-residue similarity that no length- and acidity-matched human protein matches; the loop the claim names is its weakest seven columns; and the only query that puts CD47 at the top of the proteome is one that was cut to fit CD47.
